# Simple arithmetic on a channel's own numbers is not a derived metric

Simple arithmetic on one channel's or one video's own YouTube numbers is not a derived metric. The product may add, subtract, take a share of a total, keep a running total, divide (as RPM does), take a plain average, and convert minutes to hours, as long as every input is that channel's or that video's own number from YouTube's API and no data from outside YouTube enters the calculation. The dashboard's uses are all of this kind: the share of engaged views by traffic source and by subscriber side, the running total of views over time (#57), RPM per 1,000 engaged views, the RPM to date on the RPM chart's running total, the running estimated revenue over the running engaged views, times 1,000, on the channel's or the video's own numbers (#356), RPM without other sources, ads plus YouTube Premium per 1,000 engaged views, on the channel's or the video's own numbers, with the RPM preference on (#357), watch hours from `estimatedMinutesWatched`, "Other sources" as the total less the named sources, views over engaged views on the engaged views card, one channel's or one video's own two numbers divided (#242), and the plain average of the engaged views of a channel's own newest public uploads over the same days since publish, at most 10, each counted over the window's days from its own publish day, with an upload Google has not processed for the whole window left out (#243). RPM is the channel's own reported revenue over its own reported engaged views. It reports the channel's financial performance and does not infer or project it.

A score, a rank, or any comparison with other channels stays a derived metric. A rank here means a place against other channels or a place on a computed score. Each waits for acceptance under YouTube's derived-metrics policy after the compliance audit (ADR 0004), and totals across channels stay out under ADR 0005. Sorting the creator's own videos by one of their own YouTube numbers is a sort, not a rank, and so is placing one video among the channel's own uploads by one of its own numbers: the guide lists "Top viewed videos/channels sorted by views, likes/dislikes, subscribers" among its acceptable metrics. Two uses rest on this. The video list's heading sort (#75) stays, and the latest video card's row that places the newest upload among the channel's last 10 public uploads of its format by each upload's engaged views over the same days since publish, such as "3 of 10 Shorts" (#94, #218), is a sort too. The same days are each upload's own first N days, where N is the days Google has processed for the newest upload, counting its publish day as day 1, and each upload's figure is the sum of its own days, which is simple arithmetic on that video's own YouTube numbers. That row is shown beside Studio's own "x of 10" ranking row, which stays as Studio shows it, and never replaces it.

The average of the newest public uploads (#243) is not the band of #162. A band is a range of typical performance used to classify each video as above or below it, which assigns a judgement to a video. That makes it a derived metric, so it stays out until the compliance audit (#6), and ADR 0011's benchmark rule, which allows no grade, applies to it as well. The average is one number, the sum over a count, shown as a sentence. It never classifies, ranks or colors any video, and it is never drawn on a chart. The Developer Policies Guide names averages among its acceptable simple calculations. The average picks its uploads by the rule that defines "Latest upload" in CONTEXT.md: an upload counts only when its privacy status is public.

The product never adds, averages or divides average view percentage or relative retention. Each is already a ratio YouTube computes, and combining ratios without their underlying counts gives a number YouTube never reported. Both are shown only as YouTube returns them.

The sources, read on 2026-10-08, each marked "Last updated 2026-09-14 UTC":

- The Developer Policies Guide, https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies-guide, under "Only offer metrics that are available via YouTube's API services", says: "Acceptable metrics are those that use only YouTube API data and combine them via simple mathematical calculations (combine them via addition, subtraction, averages, multiplication, division). These metrics must not incorporate any other external data sources." Its examples include "Total views in a group of videos/channels" and "Graphs visualizing raw metrics, e.g: increase in views, subscribers, likes/dislikes". The same section tells developers not to "Infer or project financial performance of a YouTube channel" or to "Calculate and assign custom "scores" to channels based on independently calculated averages or ratios".
- The Developer Policies, https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies, III.E.4.h: "Your API Clients must not (i) replace API Data with similar, independently calculated data, or (ii) access or use API Data to create new or derived data or metrics." Its example forbids using the like count "to calculate other metrics, such as the percentage of total likes that were made through your API Client or a score that factors in likes, total views, or any other API Data". The page calls itself "a legal document" and the policies "a component of the Agreement".
- The YouTube API Services Terms of Service, https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/api-services-terms-of-service, carries the note that places the guide below the legal documents: "The guide offers insight into how YouTube enforces certain aspects of the API TOS, but it does not replace any existing documents."
- The 2026 derived-metrics policy, https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/derived-metrics-policy, in its "Custom Channel Scores and Ratios" section, governs custom scores and ratios assigned to channels, such as a leaderboard ranked on a "Content Quality Score" from comment-to-view ratios, for developers who accept it.

The sources pull against each other. III.E.4.h, read literally, forbids any new or derived number, and its own example names a percentage. The guide calls simple arithmetic acceptable. The Terms of Service note says the guide does not replace the legal documents, so where the two disagree the legal text wins. The product follows the guide's line, because the guide is Google's own statement of how it enforces the policies, and it carries the risk of the wider reading knowingly. The compliance audit (ADR 0004) is where Google reviews the live product and where this line gets confirmed or corrected.

This is recorded so that the shares, sums, running totals, RPM, the RPM to date, RPM without other sources, the ratio of views to engaged views and the average of the newest public uploads are visibly a decision, not an oversight someone later "fixes" away, and so that a score slipped in beside them is visibly a breach of that decision rather than more of the same arithmetic.
